## Abstract

According to a medieval Irish legend, the ancestors of the Irish did not come from Britain or from the centre of Europe, but from Galicia. In the *Lebor Gabála Érenn*, the eleventh-century "Book of the Taking of Ireland", a king called Breogán founds the city of Brigantia and builds a tower so high that, from its top, his son Íth glimpses Ireland on the horizon. Íth sails north, is killed, and his relatives, the sons of Míl Espáine, return to conquer the island. They become the Gaels.

Few people outside Ireland and Galicia know this story, yet it has had an unusually long and active life. It was created by medieval Irish scholars from Latin books, used by Irish exiles to ask the Spanish Crown for protection, absorbed by Spanish chroniclers, and finally taken up in the nineteenth century by Galician intellectuals who were looking for a past that could distinguish Galicia from the rest of Spain. Today Breogán is in the Galician anthem, in front of the Tower of Hercules in A Coruña and on the shirt of a professional basketball club.

This essay follows the journey of the myth in both directions. It argues that the legend of Breogán tells us very little about where the Irish or the Galicians actually came from, but a great deal about how societies use origin stories when they need dignity, allies or a distinct identity.

## Research question

How did a medieval Irish origin legend become a symbol of Galician national identity, and what does its journey reveal about the way myths change when the societies that tell them change?

## Introduction

Most national myths look inward. They tell a people that they were born from their own soil, from their own heroes or from a founding act that happened at home. The legend of Breogán is stranger. It is an Irish story in which the decisive moment happens in Spain, and a Galician symbol whose oldest written versions were produced in Ireland. It belongs fully to neither place, and perhaps for that reason both have been able to use it.

The story is also an excellent example of something that is easy to forget when we talk about mythology: myths are not fixed objects inherited intact from a remote past. They are rewritten, borrowed, corrected and repurposed. Each generation reads them with its own questions in mind. A legend that served eleventh-century monks who wanted to place Ireland inside biblical history was later useful to seventeenth-century Irish nobles who needed the protection of the King of Spain, and later still to nineteenth-century Galician poets who wanted to awaken a "sleeping" nation [1-3].

Following Breogán through these moments allows us to see three different functions of the same myth: as learned pseudo-history, as diplomacy and as national identity. It also raises a contemporary question. In a period when genetic studies are regularly presented as "solving" the origins of peoples, why do we still need stories like this one?

## The story as the Irish told it

The *Lebor Gabála Érenn* (often translated as "The Book of Invasions" or "The Book of the Taking of Ireland") is a vast and complex compilation. Its original version was probably written in the second half of the eleventh century, and it survives in several later recensions that expand, correct and contradict one another [1,3]. It presents itself as a history of Ireland from the Creation onwards, describing a sequence of peoples who arrived on the island one after another: Cessair, Partholón, Nemed, the Fir Bolg, the Tuatha Dé Danann and, finally, the Gaels.

The Gaels' journey is long and deliberately biblical. Their ancestors are linked to the Tower of Babel, to Egypt and to Scythia, and they wander for centuries before reaching the Iberian Peninsula, which they conquer. There, one of their leaders, Breogán, founds a city called Brigantia and builds a great tower [4]. In one of the best-known versions, it is from the top of this tower, "on a clear winter's evening", that Ireland is first seen [5]. Breogán's son Íth sails to the island with a small company, impresses its rulers with his judgement and is then killed by them out of fear and envy [5].

His death sets the final invasion in motion. The sons of Míl Espáine, Íth's relatives, sail from Brigantia with a fleet, defeat the Tuatha Dé Danann and divide the island between them. From them descend the Gaelic dynasties of Ireland, and from Míl comes the name by which the Irish would later describe themselves in learned and poetic language: the Milesians [1,4].

Read today, the story has the shape of a foundation epic: a promised land seen from afar, a martyr whose death justifies conquest, and a group of brothers who become the ancestors of a nation. What makes it unusual is its geography. The last stop before Ireland is not Britain, which is visible from the north-east of the island, but north-western Spain.

## Where the story really came from

For centuries, the Spanish origin of the Gaels was accepted as history by Irish poets and scholars. Modern scholarship, however, has shown that it is not a fragment of ancient oral memory but a learned construction, assembled by medieval writers from the Latin books available to them [1,3].

The first clue is in the name of the hero. Míl Espáine is not an Irish name at all; it is an Irish rendering of the Latin *miles Hispaniae*, "the soldier of Spain" [3]. An earlier text, the *Historia Brittonum* or "History of the Britons", written in the early ninth century, already speaks of the "sons of a Spanish soldier" who reached Ireland, and these figures are clearly the forerunners of the sons of Míl [3].

The second clue is the tower. In the early fifth century, Paulus Orosius, a priest from Gallaecia, wrote a widely read universal history that included a geographical description of the known world. In it he describes Brigantia, a city of Gallaecia, as raising a very high lighthouse "to keep watch on Britain", and he places Ireland between Britain and Spain, with its nearer coasts facing towards Brigantia across a great distance of sea [3,6]. Orosius does not say that Ireland can be seen from the tower. But an Irish reader who knew this passage could easily make the leap from a tower that "watches" towards the north to a tower from which Ireland was first glimpsed [3]. Other names in the Irish account, such as the estuary of Scéne, also appear to come from Orosius rather than from native tradition [3].

The third clue is a famous piece of medieval etymology. Isidore of Seville, who died in 636, suggested that *Hibernia*, the Latin name for Ireland, came from *Iberia*. The two names are in fact unrelated: Hibernia derives from a Celtic form of the island's name, and its resemblance to both Iberia and the Latin word for "wintry" is coincidental [3]. For early Irish scholars, however, Isidore was an authority, and the apparent similarity between Iberia and Hibernia, together with the similarity between *Galicia* and *Gael*, seemed to confirm that the two lands were connected [3,4]. Learning from Visigothic Spain was highly respected in early medieval Ireland, which made the idea even more plausible to the monks who copied and expanded these texts [3].

Seen in this light, the legend of Breogán is not a memory of a prehistoric migration but what the Celticist John Carey has called "synthetic pseudohistory": an attempt by Christian scholars to give Ireland a place in the history of the world as it was known from the Bible and from classical authors [1]. Ireland lay at the edge of that world. By making the Irish descendants of biblical peoples who had passed through Egypt and Spain, the compilers connected their island to the centre of sacred and imperial history. Spain was the obvious final step, because it was the part of the continent that seemed closest to Ireland on the mental maps of the time [3].

## A lighthouse becomes a tower

The tower that Orosius described was real. The Roman lighthouse of Brigantium, known today as the Tower of Hercules, was built at the end of the first century on a peninsula near what is now the centre of A Coruña [7,8]. It is the only Roman lighthouse that is still fully preserved and still used for maritime signalling, and it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2009 [7]. Until the twentieth century it was commonly called the *Farum Brigantium*, the lighthouse of Brigantia [7].

Because of its age and its exposed position on the Atlantic, the tower has always attracted stories. Its current name comes from a different legend, transmitted in Castile by the thirteenth-century chronicle of Alfonso X, in which Hercules defeats the giant Geryon after a long fight and builds a tower over his buried head [8,9]. A third tradition, the Latin tale of the monk Trezenzonio, tells of a Galician who climbs the tower of Brigantia, sees a marvellous island in the ocean and sails to it [10]. The date of this text is debated, but it shows that the idea of a tower from which a distant island can be seen was also present in Galician learned culture [10,11].

The Irish legend, in other words, did not arrive in an empty space. It joined a monument that was already overloaded with meaning. From the sixteenth century, Spanish chroniclers such as Florián de Ocampo began to repeat the story of Breogán, and the city council of A Coruña notes that its diffusion was later encouraged by the Irish Colleges founded in Spain, particularly the one in Santiago de Compostela [9,12]. By the time Galician historians took an interest in it, Breogán's tower and the Tower of Hercules had become, for many readers, the same building.

## The myth as diplomacy: Irish exiles in Spain

The second life of the legend began in a period of crisis. At the end of the sixteenth century, the Gaelic lords of Ireland were fighting a losing war against the English Crown, and Catholic Spain was their most important potential ally. In 1601, a Spanish force landed at Kinsale to support them; the defeat that followed marked the beginning of the end of the old Gaelic order [12,13].

In this context, the Milesian legend stopped being only a matter of learned history and became a diplomatic argument. Gaelic leaders wrote to the Spanish king presenting themselves not as foreigners asking for help but as distant kin returning to their original home. Donal Cam O'Sullivan Beare, one of the last chieftains to resist, appealed to the Spanish Crown by invoking the Spanish descent of his people [12]. Reports presented at the Spanish court by Irish exiles in the early seventeenth century consistently traced their origins to the Spanish king Milesius [14].

The argument had practical consequences. After Kinsale and the departure of many Gaelic nobles from Ireland, thousands of Irish soldiers, clerics and families settled in the Spanish monarchy. Spanish officials could describe them as "our Spanish brethren", and they enjoyed privileges and opportunities that other foreigners did not [12,15]. Irish Colleges were founded under the patronage of the Spanish Crown, beginning with Salamanca in 1592, to train Catholic priests at a time when this was impossible in Ireland itself [12]. Philip O'Sullivan Beare, Donal Cam's nephew, was educated in Santiago de Compostela and wrote in Latin about the history and dignity of the Irish people from exile [14].

The myth also continued to grow inside Ireland. In the seventeenth century, Geoffrey Keating's *Foras Feasa ar Éirinn*, a history written in Irish to defend the country's civilisation against English contempt, retold the Milesian story at length [12]. In this phase the legend served two purposes at once: it gave the Irish an ancient and noble lineage at a moment of humiliation, and it gave them a reason to expect solidarity from a powerful Catholic monarchy. The tower in Brigantia had become a bridge.

## The myth comes home: Galician Celticism

The third life of the legend took place in Galicia itself, in the second half of the nineteenth century. This was the period of the *Rexurdimento*, the "resurgence" of Galician language and literature after centuries in which Castilian had dominated public, written and prestigious culture [2,16]. Writers such as Rosalía de Castro showed that Galician could be a language of serious literature. Historians and poets also began to ask a political question: if Galicia had its own language and culture, did it also have its own history as a people?

The answer that many of them found was Celtic. Earlier antiquaries had already suggested that the inhabitants of ancient Gallaecia were Celts, and the hillforts of the Iron Age, the *castros*, were interpreted as Celtic settlements [17,18]. The historian Manuel Murguía, one of the founding figures of Galician nationalism, and the writer Benito Vicetto developed this idea into a full narrative of Galician origins, drawing both on older local scholarship and on Irish and French studies of the Milesian legend [16]. The Irish story of Breogán was particularly attractive: it did not simply say that Galicia had once been Celtic, it made Galicia the departure point of one of the most prestigious Celtic peoples in Europe.

The usefulness of this narrative is easy to understand. Scholars of Galician nationalism have described Celticism as a *mythomoteur*, a founding myth around which a collective identity could be organised [19]. It distinguished Galicia from a Spanish national narrative centred on Castile, Rome and the Reconquest. It connected Galicia to the Atlantic world, to Ireland, Scotland, Wales and Brittany, rather than to the Mediterranean. And it offered what one study calls a "symbolic recovery" of dignity for a society marked by poverty, emigration and political marginalisation [16]. Like many nineteenth-century national movements across Europe, this Celticism also carried ideas about race and ancestry that historians now examine critically [20].

The poet who gave this myth its most lasting form was Eduardo Pondal (1835-1917). Influenced by Murguía and by the Ossianic poetry that was fashionable across Europe, Pondal imagined himself as a bard of an ancient Celtic people and filled his collection *Queixumes dos pinos* (1886) with warriors, harps, oak forests and references to Breogán [2,16,21].

## An anthem that never names Galicia

In 1890, the choral society Orfeón Coruñés, directed by the composer Pascual Veiga, organised a competition for a Galician "regional march". The lyrics chosen were a poem by Pondal, originally titled "Breogán" and later known as "Os pinos" ("The Pines"), set to music by Veiga [2,22]. The anthem was first performed in Havana in 1907, where a large Galician emigrant community lived, and it spread from the Americas back to Galicia [22,23]. After decades of informal use, it was officially adopted as the anthem of Galicia during the democratic transition, in 1977 [23,24].

The text of "Os pinos" has a remarkable feature: it never mentions Galicia by name. Instead, the land is addressed through Breogán. It is the *fogar de Breogán*, the "home of Breogán", and the *nazón de Breogán*, the "nation of Breogán" [4,25]. In the full poem, Pondal multiplies the formula, ending stanza after stanza with variations such as the people, the daughters and the harps of Breogán [25]. The legendary king of the Irish book has become the personification of Galicia itself.

This is perhaps the most striking transformation in the whole history of the myth. In the *Lebor Gabála*, Breogán is a secondary character, important mainly because his tower allows Ireland to be seen and because his descendants leave for it. In Pondal's anthem, he is the father of the nation that stays behind. The story that once explained why the Gaels left Galicia now explains why Galicia should remain itself.

## What archaeology and genetics say

From a scientific point of view, the legend of Breogán cannot be taken as a historical record. As shown above, its key elements come from Latin geography and etymology rather than from inherited memory [1,3]. Galician archaeologists and historians have also spent decades revising the nineteenth-century Celtic narrative. The castro culture of north-western Iberia is now studied on its own terms, and the image of a single Celtic invasion that created the Galician people has been largely abandoned in favour of more gradual and regional explanations [17,18].

The legend nevertheless returned to the news in the twenty-first century through genetics. In 2006, Oxford researchers such as Stephen Oppenheimer and Bryan Sykes argued that a large part of the ancestry of the population of Britain and Ireland went back to people who had recolonised these islands after the last Ice Age from refuges in the south, including the Iberian Peninsula [26]. Popular articles quickly summarised this as "the Irish come from Spain", and the old Milesian story suddenly seemed to have been confirmed.

Later research made the picture far more complex. The first ancient Irish genomes, published in 2016, showed at least two major population movements into Ireland: early farmers whose ancestry ultimately came from the Near East, and later Bronze Age newcomers with substantial ancestry from the Pontic Steppe, north of the Black Sea [27,28]. Modern Irish genetic profiles are strongly related to those Bronze Age individuals. Some archaeologists and linguists, such as Barry Cunliffe and John Koch, have proposed that Celtic languages may have developed along the Atlantic façade of Europe, including Iberia, but this remains a debated hypothesis rather than an established fact [12,29].

What science suggests, therefore, is not that the Milesian legend was "true", but that Ireland and Atlantic Iberia have been connected for thousands of years by the sea, by trade and by shared cultural contacts, alongside many other routes of movement across Europe. As John Carey has argued, even if a genuine prehistoric link were one day demonstrated, the medieval legend would at most be a lucky guess, not a memory transmitted from the Bronze Age [30].

## The tower today

In contemporary Galicia, Breogán is everywhere and nowhere at once. He is rarely the subject of serious historical belief, yet he remains a familiar cultural reference. A large granite statue of Breogán by the sculptor Xosé Cid has stood at the foot of the Tower of Hercules since 1995, at the beginning of the ramp that leads visitors up to the lighthouse [31]. The professional basketball club of Lugo is called Club Baloncesto Breogán, and there is a Parque de Breogán in Madrid [4]. Galician Celtic music, bagpipes, festivals and Atlantic imagery continue to draw on the same nineteenth-century imagination, often with more pleasure than ideology.

In Ireland, meanwhile, the Milesians have largely moved from history books into mythology courses, tourist storytelling and genealogical enthusiasm. The Spanish connection remains a popular topic, partly because it offers a pleasant alternative to the long and painful relationship with Britain. For Irish visitors, the Tower of Hercules can be a surprising discovery: a place in Spain that appears in their own national legend.

The tower itself has outlived all these interpretations. It was a Roman lighthouse, a ruin, a reference point for sailors, a medieval symbol of the city, an eighteenth-century restoration project and now a World Heritage Site [7,9]. Each period has projected its own story onto it. Breogán is simply the story that has travelled furthest.

## Comparative analysis: one myth, two functions

Comparing the Irish and Galician uses of Breogán shows how the same narrative can perform almost opposite functions.

In Ireland, the legend was originally a way of explaining arrival. It answered the question "where did we come from?" in a way that connected a remote island to biblical and classical history. Later, in exile, it became a way of claiming kinship with a powerful foreign ally. Its emphasis was on movement: from Babel to Egypt, from Egypt to Spain, from Spain to Ireland, and later from Ireland back to Spain.

In Galicia, the same legend became a way of explaining permanence. It answered a different question: "what makes us different from those around us?" Galicia did not need to be connected to the centre of the world; it needed to be distinguished from the centre of Spain. Breogán therefore stopped being the ancestor of people who left and became the father of people who remained. The emphasis shifted from journey to home, from conquest to belonging.

There is also a symmetry in the way both societies used the myth in moments of vulnerability. Medieval Irish scholars wrote at the edge of Christian Europe. Gaelic exiles wrote after military defeat. Galician intellectuals wrote in a society shaped by poverty, emigration and the subordination of its language. In each case, an origin story provided something that the present did not: antiquity, nobility and a place in a larger history.

Finally, the case of Breogán shows how myths can circulate between cultures rather than belonging to a single one. The story was built in Ireland from a Galician priest's geography, carried to Spain by Irish exiles, adopted by Spanish chroniclers, reinterpreted by Galician poets and then rediscovered by Irish visitors in A Coruña. It is less a national myth than an Atlantic one.

## Final reflection

The legend of Breogán is false as history and true as cultural evidence. It does not tell us that the Irish descend from a Galician king, or that Galicia is essentially Celtic. It tells us that people at different moments needed to imagine a link between these two edges of Europe, and that they found that link in a tower looking out over the sea.

This is why the myth remains interesting. It shows that origin stories are rarely about the past alone. The medieval compilers of the *Lebor Gabála* were thinking about their place in Christian history. Irish exiles were thinking about protection and survival. Pondal and Murguía were thinking about a nation that, in their view, had to wake up. Each group read the same story and found in it the answer to its own present.

Myths change when society changes because their purpose is not to preserve facts but to make a situation meaningful. Breogán's tower has been a lookout, a departure point, a bridge and a national home. Perhaps its most lasting lesson is that identity is often built not only from what a community remembers, but also from what it decides to imagine together.

## Key takeaways

1. In the eleventh-century *Lebor Gabála Érenn*, the Gaels reach Ireland from Brigantia in Galicia, where Breogán's tower allows his son Íth to see the island.

2. The Spanish origin of the Irish is a learned medieval construction, built from Latin sources such as Orosius, the *Historia Brittonum* and Isidore of Seville, not a memory of a real migration.

3. Breogán's tower is commonly identified with the Roman lighthouse of Brigantium, today's Tower of Hercules in A Coruña, which was already surrounded by other legends.

4. In the seventeenth century, Irish exiles used the Milesian legend to present themselves as kin of the Spanish and to seek the protection of the Spanish Crown.

5. Nineteenth-century Galician intellectuals, especially Murguía and Pondal, turned Breogán into a symbol of Galician identity, and the Galician anthem calls the country the "nation of Breogán" without naming Galicia.

6. Modern archaeology and genetics do not confirm the legend, but they do show long-standing Atlantic connections. The myth's real value lies in what it reveals about how societies build identity.
