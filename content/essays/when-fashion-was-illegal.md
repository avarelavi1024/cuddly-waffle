## Abstract

For most of history, getting dressed was not only a matter of taste. In ancient Rome, medieval England, Renaissance Florence, Habsburg Spain, Edo Japan and colonial Louisiana, governments passed laws that told people which fabrics, colours, furs, jewels and garments they were allowed to wear. These rules are known as sumptuary laws, from the Latin *sumptus*, "expense".

At first sight they look like curiosities: a ban on purple, a limit on the length of a shoe, a decree against enormous collars. Read together, however, they reveal a serious idea. Clothing was treated as a public language, and those in power wanted to control who could speak it. Sumptuary laws tried to keep social hierarchies visible, protect local economies, discipline women's bodies and, in some cases, erase the identity of conquered or marginalised groups.

This essay follows that history across several societies and asks what these laws were trying to protect, why they almost always failed, and what their failure tells us about fashion today.

## Research question

What were laws that controlled clothing trying to protect, and what do they reveal about the relationship between fashion, power and identity?

## Introduction

Today, most people in Europe can wear almost anything they can afford. A student can buy a silk shirt, a nurse can wear purple, and a designer can mix luxury and sportswear without asking permission from anyone. Dress codes still exist in schools, offices and institutions, but the basic principle is clear: clothing belongs to personal choice.

Historically, this freedom is very recent. For centuries, the question was not only "what suits me?" but also "what am I allowed to wear?" Clothing was expected to reveal rank, wealth, gender, profession and sometimes religion or origin. A well-dressed stranger was not only elegant; he or she could be a social problem, because appearance was supposed to tell the truth about who someone was.

The historians Giorgio Riello and Ulinka Rublack have described sumptuary laws as a place of contestation between individuals and states, and as part of the long process through which dress became an expression of identity and, eventually, something close to a modern right [1]. That is the perspective of this essay. Sumptuary laws are not only funny episodes in the history of fashion. They show how clothing became political long before the word "fashion" meant what it means today.

## What sumptuary laws were

Sumptuary laws regulated consumption: what people could eat at banquets, how much they could spend on weddings and funerals, and above all what they could wear [2]. Clothing was their most frequent target because it was the most visible form of consumption. A feast happened indoors and ended; a gown, a fur collar or a gold chain walked through the streets every day.

These laws usually did several things at once. They limited luxury in the name of moral restraint, but they also distributed luxury according to rank: certain materials were not forbidden to everyone, only to those considered unworthy of them [2,3]. They often protected local industries by restricting expensive imported fabrics. And, especially in cities, they paid special attention to women's dress, which was frequently presented as a source of vanity, family ruin or moral danger [3,4].

Their logic can be summarised in one sentence: appearance should match position. When it no longer did, authorities saw disorder.

## Rome: purple, gold and a women's protest

One of the most famous early examples comes from ancient Rome. In 215 BC, during the Second Punic War and shortly after Rome's catastrophic defeat by Hannibal at Cannae, the Romans passed the *lex Oppia*. It prohibited women from owning more than half an ounce of gold, from wearing purple-dyed or multi-coloured garments and from riding in carriages in the city except during religious festivals [5].

The law was presented as a wartime austerity measure. Twenty years later, however, the war had been won and the restrictions remained. In 195 BC, when the repeal of the law was debated, women filled the streets and the Forum to support it. The historian Livy later reconstructed the debate: Cato the Elder defended the law as a barrier against luxury and female disorder, while the tribune Lucius Valerius argued that dress and ornament were among the few forms of recognition available to Roman women [6,7]. The law was repealed.

The episode is revealing because it already contains the main tensions of later sumptuary laws: luxury and morality, public crisis and private expression, and the special anxiety caused by women's clothing. It also introduces a colour that would carry political meaning for centuries. Tyrian purple, made from sea snails and extremely expensive, became a mark of the highest status in Rome; later Roman rulers restricted its use more and more closely [2].

## England: after the Black Death

In medieval Europe, sumptuary laws multiplied when societies became more mobile. England is a clear example. The first English act of apparel was passed under Edward III in 1337, and the last was repealed under James I in 1604 [4].

The best-known medieval statute, the Statute Concerning Diet and Apparel of 1363, described the dress allowed to each social group in detail. Its context matters. After the Black Death, the population had fallen dramatically, wages had risen and many people who had previously lived in dependence suddenly had more money. Some of them began to dress like those above them, and the law tried to stop this "confusion" of ranks [8].

The Tudor monarchs continued the tradition. Henry VIII passed acts of apparel in 1510, 1514, 1515 and 1533, linking status directly to the quality of cloth a person could wear [4]. Imported silks, furs and metal thread worked as material signs of rank. Under Elizabeth I, the rules specified, for example, that purple silk, cloth of gold and sable fur were reserved for the royal family [9]. As an article from Shakespeare's Globe puts it, if a milkmaid could not be told apart from a countess at a glance, the whole order of society seemed at risk [9].

Yet evidence of systematic enforcement is surprisingly thin [9]. The laws were repeated again and again, which suggests that people kept breaking them. By the early seventeenth century, England abandoned general sumptuary legislation altogether [4].

## The Italian cities: women's dress and the fashion police

In the city-states of medieval and Renaissance Italy, sumptuary laws were even more frequent and detailed. Genoa is often credited with one of the first, in 1157; Florence and Venice followed with their own laws in the late thirteenth century [10]. Italian cities did not usually face the same problem as monarchies, which wanted to separate nobility from commoners. Their merchants and bankers were themselves rich and powerful. For that reason, Italian laws focused heavily on women's clothing, trains, sleeves, jewellery and the cost of weddings, and they often justified restrictions in moral terms [3,10].

Enforcement could be strikingly concrete. Cities appointed officials to monitor dress. In Florence and Siena between the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, dress regulations were reformed repeatedly, citizens were encouraged to report offences, and officers could confront people in markets, taverns and squares and confiscate forbidden jewellery or accessories [11]. Genoa had its *Magistrato delle Pompe*, an office specifically responsible for regulating ostentation [12].

At the same time, people learned to negotiate. Contemporary writers joked about Florentine women's skill as "logicians" when defending their clothes against the law: a forbidden ornament could be renamed, a cut could be reinterpreted, a garment could be argued into a different category [10]. Sumptuary law created not only prohibitions, but also creativity.

## Spain: from the ruff to the golilla

Spain had its own long tradition of sumptuary *pragmáticas*, royal decrees that tried to moderate spending on clothing, carriages, ceremonies and other luxuries. Under the Habsburgs, the Spanish court was associated with a sober, dark style of dress that was admired across Europe, but this sobriety coexisted with expensive fabrics, embroidery and accessories [13,14].

The most visible accessory was the ruff, the large pleated collar known in Spanish as *gorguera* or *lechuguilla*. Over the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, ruffs grew larger and more elaborate, requiring fine linen, starch, care and time [14]. In 1623, during the early reign of Philip IV and under the influence of the Count-Duke of Olivares, a new set of sumptuary *pragmáticas* tried to reform customs and reduce luxury. Among other measures, the great ruffs were replaced by the *golilla*, a stiff, plain collar that became a distinctive feature of Spanish male dress [13,15].

The reforms did not end luxury: later monarchs kept issuing new *pragmáticas* against excess in dress for more than a century [16]. What did last was the golilla. That is exactly what makes the case interesting. A law that wanted to restrict fashion ended up creating a new fashion, one that today we immediately recognise in the portraits of Velázquez. Sumptuary law was not only a brake on style; sometimes it became style.

Spain also offers a reminder that sumptuary rules concerned more than luxury. Another *pragmática* of Philip IV's reign tried to forbid the *tapadas*, women who covered their faces with veils in public. The concern was not only modesty, but also the possibility that hidden faces made social control more difficult [17].

## Japan: the art of hidden luxury

Sumptuary laws were not a European invention. In Japan during the Edo period (1603-1868), the Tokugawa shogunate repeatedly issued regulations meant to preserve a rigid social order. Merchants, who were formally placed below samurai and farmers in the official hierarchy, were becoming rich, and their visible spending was seen as a threat to that order [18,19].

The response of Edo's merchants is one of the most elegant episodes in fashion history. Instead of displaying wealth on the outside, many began to wear plain, dark or subdued outer garments and to place their luxury where it was less visible: in silk linings, under-kimono and carefully chosen details [19]. This culture of restraint and refinement contributed to the aesthetic ideal known as *iki*, an understated, knowing elegance addressed more to peers than to the public [19].

The parallel with contemporary "quiet luxury" is obvious. A plain coat with an extraordinary lining, or a sweater whose quality only experts recognise, follows the same logic: status is still communicated, but only to those who know how to read it. Edo Japan shows that restricting display does not eliminate distinction. It can make distinction more subtle.

## When dress becomes identity: Ireland, Scotland and Louisiana

Some laws about dress were not mainly concerned with luxury at all. They targeted identity.

In Ireland, the Statutes of Kilkenny of 1366 tried to stop English settlers from becoming "Irish". The statutes complained that many English in Ireland had abandoned the English language, manners, way of riding and apparel, and they prohibited the adoption of Irish customs, including Irish dress [20,21]. In 1537, under Henry VIII, the Irish parliament passed "An Act for the English Order, Habit and Language", which again presented Irish dress, hair and language as obstacles to English rule [22]. Garments such as the long mantle and the saffron-dyed shirt became political symbols, and historians have studied these regulations as part of the colonial effort to define who belonged to which community [23].

In Scotland, after the defeat of the Jacobite rising at Culloden, the Dress Act of 1746 prohibited civilian men and boys in the Highlands from wearing Highland clothes, including the kilt and plaid, and from using tartan for coats; soldiers in Highland regiments were exempt [24,25]. The ban was repealed in 1782. By then, Highland dress had changed meaning: it was later revived, romanticised and transformed into a national symbol, often by wealthier Scots and by the British monarchy itself [25].

In colonial Louisiana, under Spanish rule, Governor Esteban Miró issued regulations in 1786 that required free women of colour in New Orleans to cover their hair with a headwrap, the *tignon*, and to avoid "excessive attention to dress". The intention was to mark these women as socially inferior and to limit their visibility. Instead, many turned the tignon into an elaborate and fashionable accessory, using fine fabrics and creative knots. As the historian Carolyn Morrow Long has noted, the tignon became less a badge of dishonour than a fashion statement [26].

These cases show the darkest side of dress regulation. Clothing can be used to separate colonisers from colonised, loyal from rebellious, and "respectable" from "dangerous". They also show resilience: communities often transformed the very garments that were imposed or forbidden into symbols of dignity.

## Did they work?

The short answer is: rarely, and never completely. The philosopher and historian of science Lorraine Daston has described sumptuary laws as an extreme case of "rule failure" [27]. They were repeated because they were broken. They were broken because fashion moved faster than legislation, because enforcement was expensive and unpopular, and because those with money always found new ways to express status.

There were several reasons for this failure. First, clothing was too complex to classify. Every new fabric, cut or ornament forced authorities to update the list of what was allowed. Second, the same elites who wrote the laws often benefited from luxury trades. Third, people were skilled at interpretation: Florentine women renamed garments, Edo merchants moved luxury inside, and Creole women in New Orleans turned a mark of inferiority into a crown [10,19,26].

Even so, sumptuary laws were not irrelevant. They shaped the meaning of colours and materials, encouraged certain styles, created new forms of discretion and left behind a rich archive of what societies feared. In that sense, their failure was productive. By trying to fix appearance, they revealed how unstable appearance had become.

## Why it still matters today

Formal sumptuary laws have almost disappeared in Europe, but the questions behind them have not. School uniforms, workplace dress codes, rules in religious or ceremonial spaces and debates about what is "appropriate" in public all show that clothing remains a social matter.

The market has also taken over part of the old function of sumptuary law. Today, price often does what legislation once tried to do. Luxury brands create exclusivity through cost, limited editions, waiting lists and recognisable codes. At the same time, "quiet luxury" reproduces the logic of Edo merchants: wealth is signalled through cut, fabric and fit rather than through obvious logos. Fast fashion, by contrast, has made the appearance of luxury cheaper and more accessible than ever, blurring the visual signs of class that sumptuary laws once tried to protect.

Perhaps the most important legacy is the idea that dressing is a form of expression that belongs to the individual. Riello and Rublack's work suggests that the long conflict over sumptuary laws contributed to the modern idea of a "right to dress" [1]. When we choose a colour, a cut or a fabric without asking permission, we are using a freedom that was negotiated slowly, garment by garment, over centuries.

## Comparative analysis: four motives behind the laws

Looking across these cases, four main motives appear again and again.

The first is **hierarchy**. In Rome, England and Edo Japan, laws tried to keep rank visible. Clothing was supposed to show at a glance who deserved deference.

The second is **economy**. Restrictions on imported silks, furs and luxury goods were often meant to protect local production, prevent debt and reduce spending considered useless or dangerous [2,4].

The third is **morality and gender**. In many cities, especially in Italy, women's clothing became the main battleground. Ornament was linked to vanity, sin and family ruin, while men's power over the public order was presented as natural [3,10].

The fourth is **identity and control**. In Ireland, Scotland and colonial Louisiana, dress laws were used to separate groups, suppress cultural belonging or mark people as inferior [20-26].

These motives often overlapped. A law could protect local wool, discipline women and confirm aristocratic privilege at the same time. What united them was the belief that the visible order of clothing should reflect the invisible order of society.

## Final reflection

The history of sumptuary laws is, in many ways, the history of a losing battle. Again and again, authorities tried to fix the meaning of clothes, and again and again people found ways to move, reinterpret or subvert those meanings. Purple, silk, gold, ruffs, tartan and headwraps were not only materials or garments; they were signs in a constant negotiation between power and desire.

What makes these laws fascinating today is not their severity, but their vulnerability. They show how much clothing mattered to those in power, and how impossible it was to control completely. A society that legislates the length of a shoe or the width of a collar is a society that understands, perhaps better than we sometimes do, that appearance is never trivial.

When fashion was illegal, it was also powerful. That may be the most useful lesson for a time when we can wear almost anything: clothes still speak, even when no law tells us what they must say.

## Key takeaways

1. Sumptuary laws regulated what people could wear, eat and spend, and clothing was their most frequent target because it was the most visible form of consumption.

2. Many laws tried to keep social hierarchies visible, especially in periods of economic change, such as England after the Black Death or Japan during the rise of the merchant class.

3. In Italian cities, sumptuary laws focused strongly on women's dress and were enforced by specific officials, yet people constantly found ways around them.

4. In Spain, the 1623 reforms of Philip IV failed to stop luxury but helped create the golilla, a new and lasting fashion.

5. In Ireland, Scotland and colonial Louisiana, dress laws were used to control identity, but forbidden or imposed garments often became symbols of dignity and belonging.

6. Sumptuary laws mostly failed, but they reveal how deeply clothing was connected to power, and they help explain why the freedom to choose what we wear feels so personal today.
